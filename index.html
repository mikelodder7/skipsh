A tradeoff framework for post-quantum cryptography

The SKiP/SH
Model

Classical primitives gave you everything at once. Post-quantum schemes don't. Like CAP for databases, you pick two of the visible triangle and SKiP the third — and two hidden axes decide whether you can trust what's left.

Tier 1 · the visible triangle

SKiP — pick two, sacrifice one

Three engineering properties trade off against one another. A scheme can excel at two of them; whichever it can't deliver is the one it SKiPs.

S

Speed

Cycle cost — treated as a vector across keygen / sign / verify (or encaps / decaps), never a scalar. Falcon is fast to sign and verify but slow to keygen; FAEST is the inverse. Where a scheme lands depends on which phase your workload stresses.

K

Key size

Public + private key bytes. The UOV family pays here brutally — public keys from tens of kilobytes to over a megabyte — to buy a tiny signature.

P

Payload size

Signature bytes, or KEM ciphertext bytes. Hash- and MPC-in-the-Head schemes blow up here: SLH-DSA and FAEST run into the tens of kilobytes.

Tier 2 · the two hidden axes

S + H — what it costs you to trust

SKiP describes what a scheme looks like. Two further axes describe what it costs to trust — and they're where the bill comes due when a scheme appears to win all three visible axes at once.

S · was the “†” dagger

Sturdiness

Implementation robustness. Can it be built fast and safely? Constant-time discipline, freedom from floating-point fragility, side-channel resistance. Falcon is low-sturdiness (FP Gaussian sampling); SQIsign carries an open constant-time-signing problem; HAWK is essentially the un-daggered Falcon (integer-only); SLH-DSA is maximally sturdy (hash-only, no secret-dependent branching).

H · was the “‡” mark

Hardness

Cryptographic robustness — strength and maturity of the underlying assumption. SNOVA took the worst hit: Beullens' forgery attacks cut its margin by 2^8–2^39 and pushed some parameter sets below NIST's threshold, forcing a Round 2 rescue — it scores below MAYO, which weathered the same era of multivariate cryptanalysis (NIST IR 8610) without a parameter change. MQOM's ROM/QROM proofs are still maturing; isogeny & young lattice schemes have a thin analyst pool. SDitH, FAEST (AES-hardness), classic hash-based, and code-based schemes anchor the conservative end.

the central lesson

If a scheme seems to win all three SKiP axes, the cost hasn't vanished — it has moved off-triangle. The scheme is borrowing against Sturdiness or Hardness. The framework tells you where to look for the catch.

additional reading · how the families work

The private advantage, in plain English

Open a card for the scheme-level differences. The recurring idea is simple: the public puzzle is easy to check, while the private key supplies a shortcut that is hard to discover.

Lattice · noisy coordinates

A star field with a private map

Picture it: a regular star field with hundreds of dimensions. The public directions are all slightly inaccurate. Those tiny errors pile up, but the private map provides the shortcut needed to recover or create a valid nearby route.

ML-KEM, ML-DSA and FrodoKEM

ML-KEM: Alice hides a meeting point in noisy directions. Bob's private key removes enough error to recover the same shared secret; an outsider cannot efficiently untangle it.

ML-DSA: The private key helps produce a short, valid route to a public destination. Anyone can check the route without learning the private map.

FrodoKEM: The same noisy-chart idea on a less structured, much roomier chart. The conservative structure costs far more storage, bandwidth and work.

Lattice · NTRU-style

A tangled map with simpler secret coordinates

Picture it: the public map describes a space using tangled, awkward directions. A private coordinate system uses short, simple steps, making certain routes or mixtures manageable.

Falcon, HAWK and Sntrup

Falcon: The secret coordinates help find a very short route to the requested destination. Choosing routes with exactly the right statistical balance is compact and fast, but delicate to implement safely.

HAWK: It shares the short-secret-map intuition, but uses discrete, integer-oriented machinery instead of Falcon's delicate floating-point sampling approach.

Sntrup: A KEM cousin, not a Falcon-like signature. Picture a small secret pattern mixed around a circular track; the private inverse untangles the noisy mixture.

Code-based · error correction

A message sent through a storm

Picture it: Alice adds redundancy to a message, then deliberately damages it. The public encoder is visible, but Bob has the private high-powered repair manual for correcting that kind of damage.

Classic McEliece and HQC

Classic McEliece: A powerful, structured decoder is disguised as an ordinary-looking public code. The public repair instructions are enormous, but the damaged package is tiny.

HQC: Sparse, repeating structure shrinks the public material. Its different decoding puzzle instead needs a much larger damaged package.

Treasure-map version: think “reconstructing a deliberately damaged treasure map,” not reassembling a trillion literal pieces. The secret key decodes corrupted data.

Multivariate / UOV · quadratic equations

A teacher's edition for tangled equations

Picture it: the public key is a huge book of equations with all the variables tangled together. The private teacher's edition secretly sorts them so a signer can solve a fresh puzzle in stages. The answer sheet is easy for anyone to check.

UOV, MAYO, SNOVA and QR-UOV

UOV: The secret separates “vinegar” variables, chosen first, from “oil” variables that then become easy to solve. Hiding this split produces a huge public book and a tiny signature.

MAYO: Several smaller solution attempts are combined to satisfy the public equations. Its settings trade public-key size against signature size.

SNOVA: Compact matrix structure acts like a reusable printing template. It saves space, but the extra pattern gives cryptanalysts more structure to examine.

QR-UOV: Repeating quotient-ring structure compresses UOV's public book, with a different balance between compactness and conservative design.

MPC-in-the-Head · zero-knowledge proofs

A locked-room proof rehearsal

Picture it: the signer privately plays every actor solving a locked-room puzzle and records each view. A hash plays the unpredictable auditor, choosing most recordings to reveal. They must agree, while the hidden view protects the secret.

FAEST, MQOM and SDitH

FAEST: Proves knowledge of an AES key matching a public value, using optimized correlated views. It is a modern descendant of the basic rehearsal idea.

MQOM: The hidden knowledge is a solution to multivariate quadratic equations—the equation-book puzzle inside the rehearsal wrapper.

SDitH: The hidden knowledge is a small error pattern explaining a code syndrome—the error-correction puzzle inside the rehearsal wrapper.

Signatures grow because they carry commitments and many repeated rehearsal transcripts. The private key is the secret solution being proved, not a key that decrypts the recordings.

Hash-based · one-time authentication

A catalog of one-time wax seals

Picture it: the signer prepares one-time seals organized by a tree of fingerprints. A signature reveals a seal and the short catalog trail proving that it belongs beneath the public root fingerprint.

SLH-DSA, LMS and XMSS

SLH-DSA: Carries enough directions in each signature to work without remembering which seal was previously used. No state tracking is needed, but signatures are large.

LMS / XMSS: Use an indexed book of seals. The signer must remember the next unused page because reusing a one-time seal can break security; in return, signatures can be smaller.

Real private keys normally derive this material from secret seeds—the signer does not literally store a warehouse-sized book of seals.

Isogeny · structure-preserving maps

Secret bridges between unusual islands

Picture it: every mathematical island has its own landscape. Special bridges preserve important features while transforming one island into another. The signer proves knowledge of a hidden bridge route without revealing it.

SQIsign

SQIsign: Gives a compact proof of a secret relationship between supersingular elliptic-curve islands. Its keys and signatures are tiny, while signing and safe implementation are demanding.

An isogeny is a mathematical map, not a physical bridge. SQIsign also should not be equated with the broken SIDH/SIKE key exchange merely because both involve supersingular isogenies.

These analogies are intentionally simple. They leave out the more complicated parts, including the complete mathematics, security proofs, failure probabilities and implementation hazards.

selection shorthand

Picking for a deployment

01 · balanced workhorse

Need all three "okay"?

ML-DSA / ML-KEM. The centroid — nothing tiny, nothing huge, fast, and high on both hidden axes. That dual-high is exactly why they're the FIPS primary picks.

02 · max the hidden axes

Long-lived roots, can't be wrong?

SLH-DSA / Classic McEliece / FAEST. Conservative assumptions, sturdy implementations. You pay in payload or key size — accept it for trust anchors.

03 · byte-minimal, debt accepted

Bandwidth is everything?

UOV / SQIsign / MAYO. Tiny on the wire — but each picks one of small-key or small-signature, never both, and pays a Hardness or Sturdiness debt (big keys, slow signing, thin margins). MAYO's two parameter sets are the same scheme sliding along that K↔P edge. Use where the savings justify the risk.

The visible triangle behaves like CAP; the two-tier structure (SKiP + S/H) is the actual insight — it predicts where a too-good scheme is hiding its cost. Standardized set: ML-KEM (FIPS 203), ML-DSA (FIPS 204), SLH-DSA (FIPS 205), FN-DSA/Falcon (draft), stateful hash LMS/XMSS (SP 800-208), plus Classic McEliece (ISO), Sntrup (ISO), FrodoKEM (ISO), and HQC (FIPS draft, 2025) as KEMs. Round 3 additional-signature candidates (per NIST IR 8610): FAEST, HAWK, MAYO, MQOM, QR-UOV, SDitH, SNOVA, SQIsign, UOV.
